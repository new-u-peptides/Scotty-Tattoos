Getting a Tattoo Abroad

Holiday tattoos go wrong for predictable reasons, and almost none of them are about the artist's skill. Here's what to check, and when to book it.

Every summer I get the same message in mid-August: someone got tattooed on day two of their holiday, spent the week in the sea, and now the piece is patchy, blown out or angry. The tattoo was usually fine. The timing was the problem, and nobody warned them.

Getting tattooed abroad is a genuinely good idea. You get access to artists you'd never reach otherwise, and a piece that carries a place as well as a design. I tattoo people who fly into Malta for exactly that reason. But holiday tattoos fail for predictable, avoidable reasons, and almost none of them are about the artist's skill.

The single biggest mistake: tattooing on day one

A fresh tattoo is an open wound for roughly the first week. It cannot go in the sea, cannot go in a pool, cannot sit in direct sun, and does not want to be sweating under a waistband on a 34-degree afternoon. Every one of those is a normal holiday activity.

So if you tattoo at the start of a beach trip, you have two options, and both are bad: cover up and skip the water for the whole holiday, or get in the sea anyway and accept a compromised tattoo. Seawater and pool chemicals on an open wound are an infection risk and a fading risk at the same time.

Book it for the last day or two instead. You fly home with it wrapped, heal through your normal routine, and the holiday stays a holiday. This one decision prevents most of the problems on this page.

A tattoo at the start of a beach holiday is a choice between the sea and the tattoo. At the end of one, it's neither.

Hygiene checks that work in any country

Standards and licensing regimes differ enormously between countries, and you won't be able to assess a foreign regulator from a hotel room. What you can do is check the things that look identical everywhere, because sterile practice is sterile practice regardless of jurisdiction:

  • Single-use needles, opened in front of you. Non-negotiable, everywhere. If the needle is already in the machine when you sit down, ask.
  • An autoclave on the premises for anything reusable — and staff who can tell you when it was last tested, without hesitating.
  • Fresh gloves put on after the artist has finished touching their phone, the door, or your paperwork.
  • Barrier film on the machine, clip cord, lamp handle and armrest. A studio that wraps everything is a studio that thinks about cross-contamination.
  • Ink decanted into single-use caps and discarded afterwards, never poured back into the bottle.
  • A consent form and a medical questionnaire. A studio that doesn't ask about your medications, allergies or conditions isn't protecting you.

None of that requires local knowledge. If a studio fails any of it, walk, however good the portfolio looks and however far you've travelled.

The language problem is a design problem

A tattoo consultation is a detailed conversation about something permanent. "Slightly thinner lines here", "rotate it about ten degrees so it follows the muscle", "I want it bolder but not heavier" — these are subtle distinctions, and they're the ones that get lost when you and the artist don't share a fluent language.

If you're booking somewhere you don't speak the language, get the design agreed in writing, with images, before you arrive. Email is slower than talking, but it leaves a record you can both point at. And be far more explicit than feels necessary: send photos of the placement, mark up references, state sizes in centimetres.

Heat, sun and salt: the three things that ruin holiday ink

Healing skin in a hot climate behaves differently from healing skin in a northern European winter, and the aftercare card you get handed may assume the latter.

Sun is the worst of the three. UV on an unhealed tattoo doesn't just risk burning damaged skin — it degrades the ink before it has settled, and the fading is permanent. Keep a fresh piece completely out of direct sun for at least two to three weeks, then use high-factor sunscreen on it for the rest of its life. Sunscreen goes on healed tattoos only; never on an open one.

Salt water and pools are an infection route while the skin is open, and chlorine is an irritant on top of that. Ten to fourteen days minimum, longer on a large or heavily shaded piece.

Sweat and friction are the quiet ones. Heat means more sweat, more moisture trapped under clothing, and more rubbing from straps and waistbands. Loose cotton, clean every day, and wash the tattoo more often than you would at home — twice a day rather than once, with unscented soap and properly dried afterwards.

Flying with a fresh tattoo

Flying home the day after a session is fine, with a few caveats. Cabin air is extremely dry, so moisturise before you board and again on landing. Keep it covered on the plane — tray tables and armrests are not clean surfaces. Don't take a brand-new piece into a long-haul flight under a tight waistband or backpack strap.

If you have a choice, leave a full day between the session and the flight. It gives the initial weeping phase time to settle, so you're not managing a leaking tattoo in an aisle seat.

What happens if something goes wrong once you're home

This is the part people don't think about. A tattoo needs a touch-up surprisingly often — ink that didn't take evenly, a spot lifted by a scab — and the artist who did it is now in another country.

Before you book, ask two questions: does the artist offer touch-ups, and what happens if you can't come back? Good studios will look at photos remotely and advise, and many will tell you honestly whether a local artist can safely touch up their work. Get the artist's direct contact, not just the studio's front desk.

And know what you're actually looking at if it goes red. Some redness, heat and swelling are normal for the first few days — telling normal healing from infection is its own subject, and worth reading before you travel rather than at 2am in a hotel room.

Book ahead, don't walk in

The walk-in holiday tattoo is the highest-risk version of all of this. You're choosing under time pressure, from whoever has a free chair, in a town where you can't tell a great studio from a tourist-strip one.

Any artist worth travelling for is booked weeks or months out, and custom work needs design time before the session. Send your enquiry before you book the flights, not after you land. It costs nothing and it's the difference between the artist you wanted and whoever was available.

If you're coming to Malta

Most of the above applies with the volume turned up, because Malta is hot, sunny and surrounded by sea — the three things healing skin likes least. Between May and September I put clients on a tighter aftercare routine than I'd use in winter, and I'd always rather tattoo you at the end of a trip than the start.

Practically: the island is small enough that any studio is reachable within about forty minutes, English is spoken everywhere, and pharmacies stock everything you'll need. The Malta page covers the studio, getting there, and what booking here involves.

And if the travelling runs the other way — you want this work but can't get to Malta — I take guest spots and private bookings elsewhere.

A tattoo you got abroad should remind you of the trip every time you look at it. Plan it properly and it will — rather than reminding you of the week you spent keeping it out of the sea.

Common questions

Is it safe to get a tattoo abroad?

Yes, as long as you check the things that are the same everywhere: single-use needles opened in front of you, an autoclave on site, fresh gloves, barrier film on the equipment, ink decanted into single-use caps, and a consent and medical form. Local licensing rules differ between countries and you won't be able to assess them as a visitor, but sterile practice looks identical regardless of jurisdiction. If a studio fails any of those checks, walk away.

When should I get a tattoo on holiday — the start or the end?

The end, almost always. A fresh tattoo cannot go in the sea or a pool and cannot sit in direct sun for the first two weeks, so tattooing on day one of a beach holiday forces you to choose between the water and the tattoo. Booking it for the last day or two means you fly home and heal through your normal routine.

Can I swim in the sea after getting a tattoo abroad?

Not for at least ten to fourteen days, and longer on a large or heavily shaded piece. While the skin is still open, seawater is an infection route and pool chlorine is an irritant on top of that. This is the single most common way holiday tattoos get ruined.

Can I fly straight after getting a tattoo?

Yes, though leaving a full day between the session and the flight is better — it lets the initial weeping phase settle. Cabin air is very dry, so moisturise before boarding and after landing, and keep the tattoo covered on the plane rather than resting it on a tray table or armrest.

What if my tattoo needs a touch-up and I've flown home?

Ask about this before you book. Good studios will look at photos remotely and advise, and many will tell you honestly whether a local artist can safely touch up their work. Get the artist's direct contact rather than just the studio's front desk, so you can reach the person who actually did the tattoo.

Should I book ahead or walk in when I'm travelling?

Book ahead. A walk-in holiday tattoo means choosing under time pressure from whoever has a free chair, in a town where you can't tell a good studio from a tourist-strip one. Any artist worth travelling for is booked weeks out, and custom work needs design time before the session — so send the enquiry before you book the flights.
