How to Choose a Tattoo Artist

Most bad tattoos aren't the work of bad artists. They're the work of good artists asked to do something outside what they're good at. Here's how to tell the difference before you book.

Sixteen years on this side of the machine has taught me that most bad tattoos aren't the result of bad artists. They're the result of a good artist being asked to do work outside what they're good at — usually because the client picked on price, proximity or follower count instead of fit.

Here's how I'd choose, if I were choosing.

1. Look at healed work, not fresh work

This is the single most useful filter, and almost nobody applies it.

Every tattoo looks sharp in the photo taken five minutes after the session. The skin is still swollen, the lines are still sitting slightly raised, and the contrast is at its absolute peak. That photo tells you the artist can execute on the day. It tells you nothing about whether the work holds.

A healed photo — taken at six weeks or six months — tells you whether the lines stayed crisp or spread, whether the dotwork held its gradient or turned to grey mush, whether the fine detail survived or closed up. Any artist doing durable work will have healed photos, because they're proud of them. If a portfolio is entirely fresh work, that's information.

Ask directly: "can I see this piece healed?" Nobody good is offended by that question.

Fresh work shows you what an artist can do. Healed work shows you what they can do that lasts.

2. Match the style, not the skill level

A brilliant realism artist will give you a mediocre mandala. Not because they lack talent — because geometric work is a different discipline with different muscle memory, different needle configurations and different rules about how line weight ages.

Tattooing isn't one skill. It's a family of related ones, and very few people are genuinely excellent at more than two or three. The artist you want is the one whose portfolio is full of the thing you're asking for, not the one who has done it occasionally.

So: find the style first, the artist second. If you want geometric work, look for portfolios that are mostly geometric. If you want fine line, look for portfolios that are mostly fine line. An artist who is honest enough to say "that's not my style, try this person" is showing you exactly the judgement you want in someone making permanent decisions about your body.

3. Read the portfolio for repetition, not highlights

Anyone can produce one exceptional piece. What you're looking for is consistency — the same standard across twenty pieces, not one showpiece surrounded by average work.

Scroll further back than the first row. Look for:

  • Line quality across many pieces. Are straights actually straight, are circles actually round, on every piece or just the featured one?
  • Placement judgement. Does the work sit with the body's anatomy, or has a flat design been stamped onto a curved surface?
  • Variety within the style. Twenty different mandalas, or the same mandala twenty times? The second means you'll get a template.
  • Photography that isn't hiding anything. Heavy filters, extreme close-crops and dramatic lighting are sometimes just aesthetics — and sometimes they're covering shaky linework.

4. The consultation tells you nearly everything

A consultation is a two-way interview, and how it runs predicts how the tattoo will go.

What a good one looks like: the artist asks more questions than you do. They want to know what the piece means, where it sits, what's already on your body, how your skin behaves, what you do for work. They push back on things that won't age well and explain why. They're honest about how many sessions it realistically needs, even when the honest answer is more expensive to hear.

What a bad one looks like: agreement with everything. An artist who says yes to every request — including the ones that will blur into a smudge in four years — isn't being accommodating, they're being uninterested. You are paying for judgement as much as execution.

5. Red flags

Some of these are dealbreakers, some are just worth noticing:

  • They'll copy another artist's design exactly. Poor practice, and usually a worse tattoo — it was drawn for a different body.
  • No deposit, no booking system, "just come down". For custom work this means no design time, which means you're getting something drawn on the spot.
  • Pressure to book today, or a discount that expires. Permanent decisions don't need urgency.
  • Vague answers on hygiene. Ask when the autoclave was last tested. Hesitation is the answer.
  • Dismissing your questions as overthinking. You're about to be marked for life.
  • Hostility toward the idea that you might get a second opinion. Confidence doesn't need to defend itself.

6. Questions worth asking

Take these to a consultation. The answers matter less than the willingness to answer:

  • Can I see this piece healed?
  • How many of these do you do in a year?
  • How will this age on this part of the body, specifically?
  • How many sessions, honestly, and how far apart?
  • What's your touch-up policy?
  • What does the quote include — design time, stencil, touch-up?
  • What would you change about my idea if it were your arm?

That last one is the most revealing question on the list.

7. Price is a signal, not a target

Searching for the cheapest tattoo in town is the most expensive decision in tattooing, because the correction costs more than the original — laser removal runs to many multiples of the tattoo's price, and a cover-up limits every design choice you'll ever make on that patch of skin.

That doesn't mean expensive equals good; plenty of overpriced mediocre work exists. It means price shouldn't be the axis you optimise on. Work out who does the style you want at the standard you want, then find out what that costs, then save if you need to. A tattoo you waited six months for beats one you could afford last Tuesday.

Ask what the quote actually covers, too. Custom design time, the stencil, and a touch-up are included by some artists and billed by others. A cheaper headline rate that excludes all three isn't cheaper.

8. Trust the gut feeling, but verify it first

Rapport genuinely matters. You may be in a chair with this person for thirty hours across a year, and a piece designed by someone who understood what you were after will be better than one designed by someone who didn't.

But do the boring checks first. Charm is easy; healed linework isn't. Verify the work, then let the gut decide between the two or three artists who passed.

If you're choosing in a place you don't know

Everything above gets harder when you're picking a studio in a city you're visiting for a week. Reviews skew toward the friendliest studio rather than the most skilled, and you can't ask anyone local. The guide to getting tattooed abroad covers the checks that still work when you're a visitor — and why booking before you fly matters more than anything else on that list.

One last thing. The artist you want may well tell you that your idea should be smaller, simpler or somewhere else on your body. That's not them being difficult. That's the whole reason you're paying them.
